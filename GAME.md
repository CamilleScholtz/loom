# GAME.md

Game design only. No implementation, no technical detail. See `TECH.md` for that.

## Pitch

A terminal-based, system-driven narrative game in the spirit of Dwarf Fortress, rendered through a single character's perspective. The player arrives in a vaguely medieval European village in the aftermath of a destabilizing event — a murder, a disappearance, a scandal — and has a finite, randomized number of days to make sense of it. Underneath the investigation, a full social simulation runs: NPCs with traits, moods, relationships, knowledge, and goals of their own. The player can pursue the case, ignore it, fall in love, start a feud, drink themselves stupid — all at the cost of time. The world keeps living whether the player engages with it or not.

Each playthrough is self-contained. New world, new character, new case. Stories are generated by the systems, not authored. The LLM provides voice — dialogue, flavor, narration — but never decides facts.

## Design Pillars

1. **Simulation owns state. AI owns surface.** Every fact in the world — who knows what, who feels what, who did what — is produced by simulation rules and stored as data. The LLM only renders that state into language. It can never invent a fact.

2. **The world is one mind's view of it.** The player only sees what their character perceives. NPCs they haven't met are anonymous descriptions. Rumors arrive partial and biased. The map and the social graph are filtered through a single mind.

3. **Emergent over authored.** No scripted scenes, no flag-triggered dialogue trees. Every story moment falls out of the interaction of systems. The author's job is to design systems that produce good moments, not to write the moments.

4. **Extensible by design.** The engine does not know its own setting. Setting, vocation, and scenario are data-driven content packs. The default ships medieval + investigator, but the same engine should run sci-fi + station-doctor by swapping content alone.

5. **One run, one story.** Anthology format. No carryover between playthroughs. Each one is fresh — new world, new protagonist, new case. This is a design choice, not a limitation.

## The Architectural Rule

The boundary between simulation and AI is hard and load-bearing.

The simulation owns:
- Every fact about the world and its history
- Who is where, doing what
- Who knows what, from whom, with what accuracy
- All emotional and relational state
- All outcomes of player actions
- All consequences

The LLM is invoked to:
- Voice NPC dialogue, given speaker traits, listener relationship, mood, knowledge, and topic
- Voice the protagonist's dialogue options in their own register
- Narrate observations and discoveries
- Generate names, flavor text, and period-appropriate texture
- Write epilogue narration grounded in the simulation's actual facts

The LLM is never invoked to:
- Decide what happened in the past or present
- Decide relationship deltas
- Decide who knows what
- Decide outcomes of actions
- Author the mystery's solution

## Setting

Default setting: **vaguely medieval European, low fantasy, no magic.** A village or small town, a few dozen named NPCs, surrounding regions of low resolution. Period texture is consistent — candles, ledgers, taverns, gossip, priests, magistrates, livestock.

The setting is data-driven content: vocabulary, name generators, item and location registries, social customs, taboos, prompt-template flavor. Other settings — sci-fi, early modern, antiquity — can be authored as content packs without engine changes.

## The Player Character

Created fresh each playthrough.

- **Vocation** — what the character does and how they get pulled into the case. Default: **investigator**. Other vocations (physician, confessor, scribe, judge) are extensible content. Vocation provides starting skills, starting contacts, and a default hook for arriving in the village.

- **Traits** — three slots, picked by the player at creation:
  - **One virtue** (Kind, Shrewd, Patient, Bold, Honest, Devout, Curious…)
  - **One vice** (Vain, Wrathful, Greedy, Cowardly, Lustful, Lazy, Suspicious…). The vice is essential — it generates complications and interesting moments.
  - **One inclination** (Romantic, Ambitious, Wanderer, Homebody, Pious, Scholarly…)

- **Background** — what the character did before the playthrough. Provides starting skills and a small initial reputation in worldgen.

Traits color which dialogue options surface, how NPCs read the character, and compatibility for friendships and romance. They never override player decisions — the final choice is always the player's.

The protagonist has the same simulation footprint as any NPC: needs, mood, skills, knowledge, relationships. The difference is who is at the wheel.

## The World and Its People

Worldgen produces a village before play begins.

- Several dozen named NPCs with traits, moods, relationships, knowledge, possessions, and a few years of generated personal history.
- Locations with schedules — NPCs are *somewhere* at any given hour, doing something.
- A social fabric of edges: who loves, hates, owes, distrusts, envies whom.
- Secrets — facts only some NPCs know.

The systems that run continuously:

- **Needs.** Hunger, sleep, safety, belonging, purpose. Drive baseline behavior.
- **Traits and values.** Personality. Weight which need wins when two conflict.
- **Mood.** A function of recent memorable events, each event tagged with emotional valence, decaying over time.
- **Relationships.** Directed edges between any two characters: affection, trust, debt, grievance. Each edge moves only because of events — never by authorial fiat.
- **Knowledge.** What each character has personally witnessed vs. been told vs. doesn't know. The single most important system. It drives every social dynamic and every investigation.
- **Reputation.** A public, slowly-decaying summary other characters use when they haven't personally met someone.
- **Goals.** NPCs pursue active intentions, not just react. A grieving widow plans to find the killer. A jealous suitor plans to embarrass a rival.

In romance specifically: **attraction, affection, and trust are independent edges.** A character can love someone they don't desire, desire someone they don't trust. Compatibility is values-based — a pious character and a hedonist generate friction even at high affection. Sexuality and preferences are generated per NPC at worldgen; some NPCs are simply not available to the player.

## The Mystery

The case is **generated by the simulation, not by the AI.**

During worldgen, the social sim runs forward for several generated years. Pressures build naturally — debts, grudges, jealousies, ambitions. At some point one of these pressures resolves into an *act*: a murder, a poisoning, a theft, a disappearance, a betrayal, a false accusation, a scandal revealed. The simulation records exactly what happened — who, when, where, observed by whom, what was left behind, what each surviving NPC now believes vs. knows vs. has been told.

The player arrives in the village in the *state* this event left behind. Suspicions are real. Lies are real. Coincidences are real coincidences. There is one true history, and it is grounded in the social fabric.

Mystery variety is wide:

- A death (murder, suspicious accident, suicide-or-was-it)
- A disappearance (fled, taken, accident, hiding)
- A theft or fraud
- A scandal (an affair revealed, a parentage in question)
- A false accusation against an innocent
- A returning stranger making claims about the past

Each kind surfaces different systems: a theft pressures the economic layer; a scandal pressures the relationship and knowledge layers; an accusation pressures reputation. The breadth of mysteries is the breadth of the systems.

## The Deadline

Every case has a deadline. The duration is randomized — some cases must be solved in a few days; some run weeks. The deadline is communicated to the player at the opening (a magistrate's return, a funeral, a departure, a trial date).

The deadline forces real choice. The player cannot open every door. Time spent at the tavern is time not spent at the apothecary. Time spent courting someone is time not spent interviewing the suspect.

When the deadline arrives, the case resolves — solved correctly, solved incorrectly, or unsolved. Each is a real outcome with real consequences. Failure is a first-class state of the game, not a reload prompt.

## The Player Loop

A day is structured as **four events.** An event is a scene: a place plus an intent.

> *Evening, the tavern. You want to talk to the miller about his daughter.*

The player goes there. The simulation produces the scene given who is present, the time, and the mood of the room. The player acts — converses, observes, listens, leaves. A scene can resolve in two minutes or stretch twenty; the structure is "stay until you choose to leave," not a fixed turn budget within a scene.

Between events, the simulation ticks forward roughly three in-world hours. NPCs go about their lives. News spreads. Suspicions update. The world the player wakes into is not the world they left.

Eating and sleeping are abstracted unless they become a problem. Survival surfaces only when relevant — poverty, illness, hiding. The player is not playing The Sims.

The player is free to ignore the case entirely. Four events a day can be spent drinking, courting, learning a trade, picking fights, making enemies — and the deadline still arrives. The game does not require engagement with the investigation. It only insists the player live with their choices.

## The Notebook

The game records what the player **observed** — what they saw, what was said to them, what they read. It does not connect facts for them. There is no clue board. There is no inference UI.

The connecting happens in the player's head — or on a piece of paper next to the terminal, or in a second terminal where they are keeping notes. This is intentional: the meta-game of taking notes and building theories *is* part of the game, the same way a real investigation lives in a detective's notebook.

A hypothesis-tracking system may be added later as an optional layer. For v1, the player is on their own to think.

## Player Input

Hybrid mode.

- **Common actions** — move, interview, observe, read, wait — are menu-driven. The engine surfaces state-aware options. The LLM writes the option text in the character's voice given their traits and mood, so the menu reads like an inner monologue rather than a list.
- **Dialogue lines** the player actually speaks are free-form text. The LLM interprets the line; the NPC responds in character based on the simulation state — their knowledge, mood, trust, and traits.

This preserves the immersion of free expression where it matters, and the predictability of menus where it does not.

## Romance and Player-Authored Stories

The case is the spine, not the cage. The player is free to author their own stories within the simulation — romance, feuds, ambitions, betrayals, friendships, descents.

All of these run on the same machinery as the investigation. Knowledge spreads. Reputations form. Relationships shift through events. Romance especially benefits from the depth: jealousy, misread signals, rivals, secrets, scandal — all fall out of the systems for free. A love affair conducted in a village where everyone watches everyone is a different thing than one conducted in private.

Player-authored stories are not separate content. They are emergent uses of the same simulation that produces the case.

## End of Playthrough

A playthrough ends when one of the following is true:

- The deadline arrives and the case resolves (correctly, incorrectly, or unsolved)
- The player declares the case closed early
- The player character dies (rare, but possible)
- The player leaves town

The ending produces a brief epilogue narrating what happened to each major NPC after the player left. Outcomes are grounded in the simulation's facts:

- Who the player accused, if anyone
- Who the actual culprit was
- What knowledge spread through the village during the playthrough
- Relationships the player built or broke

There is no carryover to the next playthrough. The next one is a new world, a new protagonist, a new case.

## Open Questions

- How many in-world days, on average, should a playthrough run? Target: 2–4 hours of real play, but in-world days vary by case.
- How large should the named NPC roster be? Initial target: 30–60.
- What does violence look like, given that it is rare but not impossible? A poisoning, a tavern brawl, a self-defense killing — these need a treatment.
- How is the player's exit from town surfaced and chosen? Voluntary "leave town" action, automatic on case close, or both?
- How does the protagonist's own mood/needs influence player-facing options when the player is at the wheel? (Middle path: surface as flavored options and refusals, never as overrides.)
